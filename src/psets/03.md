## Problem 1 [Homework]: Hückel Theory

From quantum mechanics, you're familiar with the LCAO (Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals) method for constructing molecular orbitals (MOs). Hückel theory provides a simplified version specifically for conjugated π systems, focusing on the π-electrons that largely determine these molecules' electronic properties. In this theory, each Hückel MO $\varphi_i$ is built from the $p_z$ atomic orbitals (AOs) $\phi_\mu$ of the $N$ heavy atoms in the conjugated system:

$$
\begin{equation}  
\varphi_i = \sum_{\mu=1}^{N} c_{\mu i} \phi_\mu \text{ for } i = 1, \ldots, N. 
{{numeq}}{eq:huckel_molecular_orbitals}
\end{equation}
$$

To obtain the coefficients $c_{\mu i}$, we need to solve the Schrödinger equation for each MO $\varphi_i$. Hückel theory simplifies this quantum mechanical problem by making three key approximations:

1. The atomic $p_z$ orbitals are orthogonal: $S_{\mu \nu} = \int \phi_\mu \phi_\nu \text{d}r = \delta_{\mu \nu}$
2. The interaction (resonance) integral between bonded atoms is a constant: $H_{\mu \nu} = \int \phi_\mu \hat{H} \phi_\nu \text{d}r = \beta$ if atoms $\mu$ and $\nu$ share a bond, and zero otherwise
3. All Coulomb integrals are equal: $H_{\mu \mu} = \alpha$ for all atoms

**(a) Deriving the Eigenvalue Problem**

Show that these approximations reduce the Schrödinger equation to a matrix eigenvalue problem:

$$
\bm{H} \bm{c} = \bm{c} \bm{E} 
$$

where:
- $\bm{H}$ is the Hamiltonian (Hückel) matrix
- $\bm{c}$ contains the orbital coefficients $c_{\mu i}$
- $\bm{E}$ is the diagonal matrix of orbital energies $\epsilon_i$

**Solution:**

We start with the Schrödinger equation for a single MO $\varphi_i$ with energy $\epsilon_i$:
$$
\hat{H} \varphi_i = \epsilon_i \varphi_i
$$
The MO is expressed as a linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO) $\phi_\mu$:
$$
\varphi_i = \sum_{\nu=1}^{N} c_{\nu i} \phi_\nu
$$
Substituting the LCAO expansion into the Schrödinger equation gives:
$$
\hat{H} \sum_{\nu=1}^{N} c_{\nu i} \phi_\nu = \epsilon_i \sum_{\nu=1}^{N} c_{\nu i} \phi_\nu
$$
To convert this operator equation into a set of algebraic equations for the coefficients $c_{\nu i}$, we project onto the AO basis. This is done by left-multiplying by a specific AO $\phi_\mu$ and integrating over all space:
$$
\int \phi_\mu^* \left( \hat{H} \sum_{\nu=1}^{N} c_{\nu i} \phi_\nu \right) d\tau = \int \phi_\mu^* \left( \epsilon_i \sum_{\nu=1}^{N} c_{\nu i} \phi_\nu \right) d\tau
$$
Due to the linearity of the Hamiltonian operator and the integral, we can rearrange this to:
$$
\sum_{\nu=1}^{N} c_{\nu i} \left( \int \phi_\mu^* \hat{H} \phi_\nu d\tau \right) = \epsilon_i \sum_{\nu=1}^{N} c_{\nu i} \left( \int \phi_\mu^* \phi_\nu d\tau \right)
$$
We now introduce the standard definitions for the matrix elements:
- The Hamiltonian matrix element: $H_{\mu\nu} = \int \phi_\mu^* \hat{H} \phi_\nu d\tau$.
- The overlap matrix element: $S_{\mu\nu} = \int \phi_\mu^* \phi_\nu d\tau$.

The equation becomes the secular equation:
$$
\sum_{\nu=1}^{N} H_{\mu\nu} c_{\nu i} = \epsilon_i \sum_{\nu=1}^{N} S_{\mu\nu} c_{\nu i}
$$
This is a generalized eigenvalue problem, $\bm{H}\vec{c}_i = \epsilon_i \bm{S} \vec{c}_i$. The Hückel approximation simplifies this significantly by assuming the AOs are orthogonal: $S_{\mu\nu} = \delta_{\mu\nu}$. Applying this to the right side of the equation:
$$
\epsilon_i \sum_{\nu=1}^{N} \delta_{\mu\nu} c_{\nu i} = \epsilon_i c_{\mu i}
$$
The secular equation is now:
$$
\sum_{\nu=1}^{N} H_{\mu\nu} c_{\nu i} = \epsilon_i c_{\mu i}
$$
This equation holds for each basis function $\mu=1, \ldots, N$. For a specific MO $i$, this is the standard matrix eigenvalue equation $\bm{H} \vec{c}_i = \epsilon_i \vec{c}_i$, where $\vec{c}_i$ is the column vector of coefficients for that orbital.

To express the problem for all orbitals at once, we can combine the $N$ column vectors $\vec{c}_i$ into the coefficient matrix $\bm{c}$:
$$
  \bm{c} = \begin{pmatrix}
    \vert & \vert &  & \vert \\
    \vec{c}_1 & \vec{c}_2 & \cdots & \vec{c}_N \\
    \vert & \vert &  & \vert
  \end{pmatrix}\,.
$$
Similarly, we collect the orbital energies into a diagonal matrix $\bm{E}$. Applying the Hamiltonian matrix $\bm{H}$ to the coefficient matrix $\bm{c}$ acts on each column individually:
$$
\bm{H} \bm{c} = \bm{H} \begin{pmatrix}
    \vert & \vert &  & \vert \\
    \vec{c}_1 & \vec{c}_2 & \cdots & \vec{c}_N \\
    \vert & \vert &  & \vert
  \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
    \vert & \vert &  & \vert \\
    \bm{H}\vec{c}_1 & \bm{H}\vec{c}_2 & \cdots & \bm{H}\vec{c}_N \\
    \vert & \vert &  & \vert
  \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
    \vert & \vert &  & \vert \\
    \epsilon_1\vec{c}_1 & \epsilon_2\vec{c}_2 & \cdots & \epsilon_N\vec{c}_N \\
    \vert & \vert &  & \vert
  \end{pmatrix}
$$
Now consider the product $\bm{c}\bm{E}$. Right-multiplying by a diagonal matrix scales the columns of $\bm{c}$:
$$
\bm{c} \bm{E} = \begin{pmatrix}
    \vert & \vert &  & \vert \\
    \vec{c}_1 & \vec{c}_2 & \cdots & \vec{c}_N \\
    \vert & \vert &  & \vert
  \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \epsilon_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & \epsilon_2 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & \epsilon_N \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
    \vert & \vert &  & \vert \\
    \epsilon_1\vec{c}_1 & \epsilon_2\vec{c}_2 & \cdots & \epsilon_N\vec{c}_N \\
    \vert & \vert &  & \vert
  \end{pmatrix}
$$
By comparing the two results, we arrive at the compact matrix equation:
$$
\bm{H} \bm{c} = \bm{c} \bm{E}
$$
This is the correct matrix form of the eigenvalue problem, relating the Hamiltonian, the orbital coefficients, and the orbital energies.

## Problem 2: Natural Transition Orbitals

Now let's explore what MOs can be used for in applied quantum chemistry and where they fail. In photochemistry, which is the study of the interaction of light with matter, we are typically interested in the electronically excited states of molecules. Many researchers, especially organic chemists, tend to imagine the character of an excited state as a single electron beeing excited from an occupied MO to an unoccupied MO (e.g. a HOMO-LUMO transition). The reality is, however, that a single excited state often involves dozens—or even hundreds—of occupied to virtual orbital transitions with small amplitudes. Notably, a single occupied (or unoccupied) orbital can contribute multiple times to the excited state, meaning that excitonic states are difficult to characterize in terms of MOs. We will now learn about a method that greatly simplifies analysis of these states.

**(b) Analyzing the Transition Density Matrix**

For a given excited state, the transitions of electrons from occupied to unoccupied orbitals can be organized in a transition density matrix $\bm{T}$. The elements of this matrix $T_{ij}$ denote the contribution of the $i$-th occupied orbital to the $j$-th unoccupied orbital. Accordingly, the rows denote the occupied orbitals, and the columns denote the unoccupied orbitals.

**(c) Calculating the Natural Transition Orbitals**

Being able to clearly characterize the excited state in terms of independently arising orbital contributions requires the transition density matrix to have diagonal form. Finding a basis in which $\bm{T}$ is diagonal is equivalent to finding the eigenvectors of $\bm{T}$. Because the number of occupied and unoccupied orbitals, however, can be different, $\bm{T}$ is not a square matrix. This is the case for Benzoindol, where $\bm{T}$ is a $7 \times 6$ matrix (only considering the Hückel MOs). Therefore, instead of eigenvalue decomposition, we resort to singular value decomposition (SVD):

$$
\bm{T} = \bm{U} \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^T
$$

The matrix $\bm{U}$ defines a unitary transformation from the occupied MOs to a set of new orbitals that together represent the *hole* orbital that is left by the excited electron, while $\bm{V}$ transforms the unoccupied MOs into a set of orbitals representing the excited *electron*. We call this new set of orbitals Natural Transition Orbitals (NTOs), defined as:

$$
\begin{align}
\varphi_j^{hole} &= \sum_i \bm{U}_{ij} \varphi_i \\
\varphi_j^{electron} &= \sum_a \bm{V}_{aj} \varphi_a \,,
\end{align}
$$

where $i$ and $a$ run over the occupied and unoccupied MOs, respectively. From this definition, we also see that NTOs $\varphi_j^{hole}$ and $\varphi_j^{electron}$ come in pairs, and the corresponding singular value $\sigma_j^2$ denotes the contribution of the NTO pair to the excited state.
