2.1 Eigenvalue Algorithms

Eigenvalue problems are central to many areas of chemistry, including quantum mechanics, spectroscopy, molecular vibrations, reaction networks, and data analysis. In this section, we introduce two numerical approaches for eigenvalue problems: power iteration and the QR algorithm.

Theoretical Foundations¶

For a square matrix A\bm{A}A, an eigenvector v⃗\vec{v}v is a non-zero vector whose direction is unchanged by multiplication with A\bm{A}A:

Av⃗=λv⃗ .\bm{A}\vec{v} = \lambda \vec{v} \, .Av=λv.
(12)

The scalar λ\lambdaλ is the corresponding eigenvalue. The two algorithms below use this equation in different ways. Power iteration finds one dominant eigenvector by repeated matrix-vector multiplication. The QR algorithm transforms the whole matrix until its eigenvalues can be read from the diagonal.

Eigenvalue Decomposition

If a matrix has a complete set of linearly independent eigenvectors, we can collect these eigenvectors as columns of a matrix Q\bm{Q}Q and the eigenvalues in a diagonal matrix Λ\bm{\Lambda}Λ. The matrix can then be written as

A=QΛQ−1 .\bm{A} = \bm{Q}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{Q}^{-1} \, .A=QΛQ−1.

This is called the eigenvalue decomposition of A\bm{A}A. For real symmetric matrices, the eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal, so Q−1=Q⊺\bm{Q}^{-1} = \bm{Q}^\intercalQ−1=Q⊺.

Power Iteration¶

Power iteration starts from a vector x⃗0\vec{x}_0x0​ and repeatedly multiplies it by the matrix:

x⃗k+1=Ax⃗k∥Ax⃗k∥;k=0,1,2,… .\vec{x}_{k+1} = \frac{\bm{A}\vec{x}_k}{\|\bm{A}\vec{x}_k\|}; \quad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\,.xk+1​=∥Axk​∥Axk​​;k=0,1,2,….
(13)

If the matrix has one dominant eigenvalue, meaning one eigenvalue whose absolute value is larger than all the others, the direction of x⃗k\vec{x}_kxk​ approaches the corresponding eigenvector, given the starting vector is not orthogonal to it. Though mathematically not necessary, the normalisation of the iterate by its norm is important for numerical stability. It prevents the vector from growing too large or too small, which could lead to overflow or underflow in finite-precision arithmetic.

Once the vector has converged, the eigenvalue can be estimated with the Rayleigh quotient:

λ=x⃗†Ax⃗x⃗†x⃗ .\lambda = \frac{\vec{x}^{\dag}\bm{A}\vec{x}} {\vec{x}^{\dag}\vec{x}} \, .λ=x†xx†Ax​.
(14)

This eigenvalue has the largest absolute value among all eigenvalues, and it is called the spectral radius of A\bm{A}A, denoted as ρ(A)\rho(\bm{A})ρ(A).

Convergence proof of Power Iteration

Assume that the eigenvectors of A\bm{A}A form a basis and that the eigenvalues are ordered by magnitude, ∣λ1∣>∣λ2∣≥⋯≥∣λn∣|\lambda_1| > |\lambda_2| \geq \cdots \geq |\lambda_n|∣λ1​∣>∣λ2​∣≥⋯≥∣λn​∣. The starting vector can then be written as a linear combination of eigenvectors,

x⃗0=∑i=1naiv⃗i .\vec{x}_0 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \vec{v}_i \, .x0​=i=1∑n​ai​vi​.
(15)

After repeatedly applying the matrix, we obtain

Akx⃗0=λ1k[a1v⃗1+∑i=2n(λiλ1)kaiv⃗i] .\bm{A}^{k}\vec{x}_0 = \lambda_1^k \left[ a_1\vec{v}_1 + \sum_{i=2}^{n} \left(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\right)^k a_i\vec{v}_i \right] \, .Akx0​=λ1k​[a1​v1​+i=2∑n​(λ1​λi​​)kai​vi​].
(16)

Because ∣λi/λ1∣<1|\lambda_i / \lambda_1| < 1∣λi​/λ1​∣<1 for all i>1i > 1i>1, the remaining terms become smaller as kkk increases. The direction therefore approaches the dominant eigenvector v⃗1\vec{v}_1v1​.

QR Algorithm¶

Power iteration gives only one eigenpair. If we want all eigenvalues of a matrix, we need a different idea. The QR algorithm repeatedly performs the QR decomposition of the matrix:

Ak=QkRk\bm{A}_k = \bm{Q}_k \bm{R}_kAk​=Qk​Rk​

and then multiplies the factors in reverse order:

Ak+1=RkQk .\bm{A}_{k+1} = \bm{R}_k \bm{Q}_k \, .Ak+1​=Rk​Qk​.
(17)

For normal matrices, in particular real symmetric matrices whose eigenvalues have distinct absolute values, the QR iteration approaches a diagonal matrix without changing the eigenvalues. The eigenvalues of A0\bm{A}_0A0​ can therefore be read from the diagonal of Ak\bm{A}_kAk​ for sufficiently large kkk.

The following figure shows the same idea visually. We start from a symmetric 4×44 \times 44×4 matrix and apply the QR iteration repeatedly. The colours show the matrix entries. As the iteration proceeds, the off-diagonal entries become smaller and the eigenvalues move onto the diagonal.

Normal Matrices

A matrix A\bm{A}A is called normal if it commutes with its conjugate transpose, i.e.

AA†=A†A .\bm{A}\bm{A}^\dagger = \bm{A}^\dagger\bm{A} \, .AA†=A†A.

Normal matrices include but are not limited to real symmetric, complex Hermitian, and unitary matrices.

Convergence of the QR Algorithm

The QR iteration has two separate ideas. First, each iteration preserves the eigenvalues. Second, under additional assumptions, the sequence of matrices approaches a form from which the eigenvalues can be read directly.

To see why the eigenvalues are preserved, use the QR decomposition Ak=QkRk\bm{A}_k = \bm{Q}_k\bm{R}_kAk​=Qk​Rk​. Since Qk\bm{Q}_kQk​ is unitary, we can write Rk=Qk†Ak\bm{R}_k = \bm{Q}_k^\dagger\bm{A}_kRk​=Qk†​Ak​. Therefore,

Ak+1=RkQk=Qk†AkQk .\bm{A}_{k+1} = \bm{R}_k\bm{Q}_k = \bm{Q}_k^\dagger\bm{A}_k\bm{Q}_k \, .Ak+1​=Rk​Qk​=Qk†​Ak​Qk​.

Thus, Ak+1\bm{A}_{k+1}Ak+1​ is similar to Ak\bm{A}_kAk​, and similar matrices have the same eigenvalues. Repeating this argument shows that all matrices in the QR iteration have the same eigenvalues as the initial matrix A0\bm{A}_0A0​.

The same relationship can be traced back through several iterations. The coloured terms show where identities are inserted and where the previous QR decompositions are substituted:

Ak+1=RkQk=IRkQk=Qk†QkRkQk=Qk†AkQk=Qk†Rk−1Qk−1Qk=Qk†IRk−1Qk−1Qk=Qk†Qk−1†Qk−1Rk−1Qk−1Qk=Qk†Qk−1†Ak−1Qk−1Qk=⋯=Qk†Qk−1†⋯Q0†A0Q0⋯Qk−1Qk .\begin{aligned} \bm{A}_{k+1} &= \bm{R}_k\bm{Q}_k = \textcolor{blue}{\bm{I}}\textcolor{green}{\bm{R}_k}\bm{Q}_k \\ &= \textcolor{blue}{\bm{Q}_k^\dagger\bm{Q}_k}\textcolor{green}{\bm{R}_k}\bm{Q}_k \\ &= \bm{Q}_k^\dagger\textcolor{darkcyan}{\bm{A}_k}\bm{Q}_k \\ &= \bm{Q}_k^\dagger\textcolor{darkcyan}{\bm{R}_{k-1}\bm{Q}_{k-1}}\bm{Q}_k \\ &= \bm{Q}_k^\dagger\textcolor{orange}{\bm{I}}\textcolor{darkcyan}{\bm{R}_{k-1}\bm{Q}_{k-1}}\bm{Q}_k \\ &= \bm{Q}_k^\dagger\textcolor{orange}{\bm{Q}_{k-1}^\dagger\bm{Q}_{k-1}}\textcolor{darkcyan}{\bm{R}_{k-1}\bm{Q}_{k-1}}\bm{Q}_k \\ &= \bm{Q}_k^\dagger\textcolor{orange}{\bm{Q}_{k-1}^\dagger}\textcolor{brown}{\bm{A}_{k-1}}\textcolor{darkcyan}{\bm{Q}_{k-1}}\bm{Q}_k \\ &= \cdots \\ &= \bm{Q}_k^\dagger\bm{Q}_{k-1}^\dagger\cdots\bm{Q}_0^\dagger \textcolor{red}{\bm{A}_0} \bm{Q}_0\cdots\bm{Q}_{k-1}\bm{Q}_k \, . \end{aligned}Ak+1​​=Rk​Qk​=IRk​Qk​=Qk†​Qk​Rk​Qk​=Qk†​Ak​Qk​=Qk†​Rk−1​Qk−1​Qk​=Qk†​IRk−1​Qk−1​Qk​=Qk†​Qk−1†​Qk−1​Rk−1​Qk−1​Qk​=Qk†​Qk−1†​Ak−1​Qk−1​Qk​=⋯=Qk†​Qk−1†​⋯Q0†​A0​Q0​⋯Qk−1​Qk​.​

If A0\bm{A}_0A0​ is diagonalisable, we can insert the exact but unknown eigenvalue decomposition A0=QΛQ−1\bm{A}_0 = \bm{Q}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{Q}^{-1}A0​=QΛQ−1 into the last line:

Ak+1=Qk†Qk−1†⋯Q0†Q⏟:=PkΛQ−1Q0⋯Qk−1Qk⏟=Pk−1=PkΛPk−1 .\bm{A}_{k+1} = \underbrace{\bm{Q}_k^\dagger\bm{Q}_{k-1}^\dagger\cdots\bm{Q}_0^\dagger\textcolor{red}{\bm{Q}}}_{:=\bm{P}_k} \textcolor{red}{\bm{\Lambda}} \underbrace{\textcolor{red}{\bm{Q}^{-1}}\bm{Q}_0\cdots\bm{Q}_{k-1}\bm{Q}_k}_{=\bm{P}_k^{-1}} = \bm{P}_k\textcolor{red}{\bm{\Lambda}}\bm{P}_k^{-1} \, .Ak+1​=:=Pk​Qk†​Qk−1†​⋯Q0†​Q​​Λ=Pk−1​Q−1Q0​⋯Qk−1​Qk​​​=Pk​ΛPk−1​.

This expression again shows that the QR iteration changes the basis in which the matrix is represented, but not the eigenvalues.

The remaining question is whether the sequence actually converges. For normal matrices with eigenvalues of distinct magnitude, it can be shown that the “vanilla” QR iteration presented here converges to a diagonal matrix.[1]

For general matrices, some more conditions are needed to guarantee convergence, and the convergence proof is more involved. In this case, the QR iteration converges to an upper triangular matrix:

lim⁡k→∞Ak=(λ1⋯∗0⋱⋮00λn) .\lim_{k\to\infty} \bm{A}_k = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & \cdots & * \\ 0 & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \lambda_n \end{pmatrix} \, .k→∞lim​Ak​=⎝⎛​λ1​00​⋯⋱0​∗⋮λn​​⎠⎞​.

The eigenvalues can again be read from the diagonal.

For a normal starting matrix, in the convergent case, the accumulated product

Zk=Q0Q1⋯Qk−1Qk\bm{Z}_k = \bm{Q}_0\bm{Q}_1\cdots\bm{Q}_{k-1}\bm{Q}_kZk​=Q0​Q1​⋯Qk−1​Qk​

approximates the matrix of eigenvectors. For a non-normal starting matrix, additional work is needed to recover eigenvectors from the triangular form.

Implementation¶

Note

The following implementations are designed for real matrices. For complex matrices, some minor adjustments are needed, such as using the conjugate transpose instead of the transpose.

We start by importing NumPy:

import numpy as np

Power Iteration¶

The function below follows the power-iteration update. The input matrix is called a, the tolerance is called tol, and maxiter prevents the loop from running forever.

def power_iteration(a, tol=1e-10, maxiter=1000, seed=0):
    n = a.shape[0]
    
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = rng.random(n)
    x = x / np.linalg.norm(x)

    for niter in range(1, maxiter + 1):
        x = a @ x
        x = x / np.linalg.norm(x)
        lambda_ = x @ a @ x / (x @ x)
        residual = np.linalg.norm(a @ x - lambda_ * x)
        if residual < tol:
            break
    else:
        print('Warning: Power iteration did not converge.')

    return lambda_, x, niter

The implementation starts by obtaining the dimension of the matrix and creating a random starting vector x of the same dimension. To be more specific, we use a pseudo-random number generator (PRNG) with a fixed seed to ensure reproducibility.

Pseudo-random Numbers

Computers usually do not generate truly random numbers. Instead, they use algorithms that produce numbers which appear random. Such numbers are called pseudo-random numbers. A pseudo-random number generator starts from an initial value called a seed. If the same seed is used again, the generator produces the same sequence of numbers. This is useful in scientific computing because it makes numerical experiments reproducible.

After creating the random vector, we normalise it to have a unit norm. Then, we enter the main loop, which performs the power iteration update. In each iteration, we update the vector x according to Eq. (13). We also calculate the Rayleigh quotient lambda_ according to Eq. (14). The convergence is checked by comparing both sides of the eigenvalue equation, Eq. (12). In the code, this is done by computing the norm of the residual [ \bm{A}\vec{x} - \lambda \vec{x}. ] If this norm is smaller than the specified tolerance tol, the loop stops early. Otherwise, it continues until maxiter iterations are reached and prints a warning message. The function returns the approximate dominant eigenvalue, the corresponding approximate eigenvector, and the number of iterations performed.

The @ Operator

In Python, the @ operator is the recommended operator for matrix multiplication.[2] For NumPy arrays, this is equivalent to using np.matmul. The exact meaning of @ depends on the dimensions of the objects involved. However, when used with matrices and vectors, one can usually just put it between the two operands, as in a @ x, and read it as the usual linear algebra product.

Warning

This is different from the * operator, which performs element-wise multiplication.

See Also

The np.dot function can also be used for matrix multiplication, but it behaves differently for higher-dimensional arrays.

QR Algorithm¶

The QR algorithm is also a direct translation of the mathematical description: compute the QR decomposition, multiply the factors in reverse order, and repeat until the lower triangle is sufficiently small. Compared with power iteration, the main difference is that we update the whole matrix, not only a single vector.

def qr_algorithm(a, tol=1e-10, maxiter=1000):
    a_k = a.copy()
    n = a.shape[0]
    vecs = np.eye(n)

    for niter in range(1, maxiter + 1):
        q_k, r_k = np.linalg.qr(a_k)
        a_k = r_k @ q_k
        vecs = vecs @ q_k

        lower_triangle = np.tril(a_k, k=-1)
        if np.max(np.abs(lower_triangle)) < tol:
            break
    else:
        print('QR algorithm did not converge.')

    eigvals = np.diag(a_k)

    return eigvals, vecs, niter

The implementation starts by copying the input matrix to a_k. This keeps the original matrix unchanged and gives us a variable that represents the current matrix Ak\bm{A}_kAk​ in the QR iteration. We also create vecs as an identity matrix. During the loop, this matrix accumulates the factors Qk\bm{Q}_kQk​ and can therefore be used to approximate eigenvectors in the convergent symmetric case.

Inside the loop, np.linalg.qr(a_k) computes the decomposition Ak=QkRk\bm{A}_k = \bm{Q}_k\bm{R}_kAk​=Qk​Rk​. The line a_k = r_k @ q_k then implements the defining step of the QR algorithm, Eq. (17), by multiplying the factors in reverse order. At the same time, vecs = vecs @ q_k stores the product of all orthogonal matrices encountered so far.

The convergence criterion uses the strict lower triangle of a_k, obtained with np.tril(a_k, k=-1). For a symmetric matrix, the QR iteration should approach a diagonal matrix, so all entries below the diagonal should become small. We therefore stop once the largest absolute value in the strict lower triangle is below the tolerance tol. If this does not happen within maxiter iterations, the function prints a warning. Finally, the approximate eigenvalues are read from the diagonal of the final matrix with np.diag(a_k). The function returns these approximate eigenvalues, the accumulated matrix vecs, and the number of iterations performed.

Application: Reaction Fate Networks¶

We now return to a chemical example. Consider a repeated reaction and recycling workflow with four material pools:

  • S: starting material

  • P: desired product

  • B: byproduct

  • R: recovered material

After one cycle, each pool is redistributed over the four pools. For example, some starting material becomes product, some becomes byproduct, and some is recovered. We store these fractions in a transition matrix. Each column describes where material from one pool goes after one cycle.

labels = np.array(["S", "P", "B", "R"])

transition = np.array([
    [0.10, 0.00, 0.05, 0.80],  # to S
    [0.62, 0.92, 0.02, 0.05],  # to P
    [0.20, 0.03, 0.78, 0.05],  # to B
    [0.08, 0.05, 0.15, 0.10],  # to R
])

transition.sum(axis=0)

The column sums are all equal to one, so the total amount of material is conserved in this simplified model.

If we start from pure starting material, the composition after one cycle is given by transition @ x. Repeating this update is exactly the same matrix-vector multiplication used in power iteration.

x = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0])

for _ in range(30):
    x = transition @ x

stationary_from_cycles = x
stationary_from_cycles

The result is a composition that barely changes under another cycle. In other words, it is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 1:

transition @ stationary_from_cycles

We can also compute this stationary composition with our power iteration function. Since power iteration normalises with the Euclidean norm, we divide the final vector by its sum to obtain material fractions.

lambda_pi, x_pi, niter_pi = power_iteration(transition, tol=1e-12)
stationary_power = x_pi / x_pi.sum()

lambda_pi, niter_pi, stationary_power

Finally, we compare the result with NumPy’s general eigenvalue solver, np.linalg.eig. This function works for any square matrix, so it is the appropriate choice for the transition matrix, which is not symmetric. It returns both eigenvalues and eigenvectors; here we select the eigenvector whose eigenvalue is closest to 1.

eigvals, eigvecs = np.linalg.eig(transition)
idx = np.argmin(np.abs(eigvals - 1.0))

stationary_eig = eigvecs[:, idx].real
stationary_eig = stationary_eig / stationary_eig.sum()

print("eigenvalue from power iteration:", lambda_pi)
print("eigenvalue from eig:", eigvals[idx])
print()
for label, value_pi, value_eig in zip(labels, stationary_power, stationary_eig):
    print(f"{label}: {value_pi:6.3f}  {value_eig:6.3f}")

The two methods give the same stationary composition. The chemical interpretation is that repeated reaction and recycling cycles approach a stable distribution of material over the four pools. In this simplified example, that distribution is not derived from rate laws; it is a consequence of repeatedly applying the same transition matrix.

Testing the QR Algorithm¶

The QR algorithm is a general eigenvalue algorithm, so we test it on a matrix where the reference result is unambiguous. A molecular distance matrix can be a useful descriptor of molecular geometry, and in methods such as classical multidimensional scaling, eigenvalues of transformed distance matrices have a clear geometric interpretation. The eigenvalues of a raw distance matrix, however, usually do not correspond directly to physical observables such as energies or vibrational frequencies. For an algorithmic test, we therefore use a random symmetric matrix.

np.linalg.eigh

np.linalg.eigh is NumPy’s specialised eigenvalue solver for real symmetric or complex Hermitian matrices. Since the test matrix is symmetric, eigh is the appropriate reference here. Compared with the more general eig, it is usually faster and more stable for this class of matrices, and it guarantees real eigenvalues for real symmetric input.

rng = np.random.default_rng(12)

n = 6
a = rng.normal(size=(n, n))
a = 0.5 * (a + a.T)

eigvals_qr, eigvecs_qr, niter_qr = qr_algorithm(a)
eigvals_ref, eigvecs_ref = np.linalg.eigh(a)

print("QR eigenvalues:")
print(eigvals_qr)
print()
print("eigh eigenvalues:")
print(eigvals_ref)
print()
print("iterations:", niter_qr)

The eigenvalues agree with np.linalg.eigh.

np.allclose(eigvals_qr, eigvals_ref)

Eigenvectors have an arbitrary sign, so we compare their absolute pairwise overlaps. If both algorithms return the same eigenvectors in the same order, the absolute overlap matrix should be close to the identity matrix.

overlap = np.abs(eigvecs_qr.T @ eigvecs_ref)
overlap

np.allclose(overlap, np.eye(n), atol=1e-6)

The self-implemented QR algorithm is useful for understanding the method, but for real scientific work we should use the library implementation. NumPy’s eigh uses algorithms that are more robust and efficient than the basic unshifted QR iteration implemented here.
